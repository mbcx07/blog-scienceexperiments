The Science of Capillary Action: Water's Invisible Climb

2026-05-13 · Experiments · Tags: experiments, The, scienceexperiments

Have you ever watched a paper towel soak up a spill and wondered why the liquid seems to defy gravity, creeping upward against the pull of the Earth? Or marveled at how a tall tree can transport water from its roots to its highest leaves, sometimes over a hundred meters above the ground? The answer lies in a fascinating phenomenon called capillary action—a force that is both invisible and incredibly powerful. In this article, we'll dive into the science behind capillary action, explore hands-on experiments that bring it to life, and uncover the surprising ways it shapes our world. Whether you're a curious kid, a dedicated teacher, or a lifelong learner, these activities will turn a simple concept into a memorable discovery.

What Is Capillary Action?

Capillary action is the ability of a liquid to flow in narrow spaces without the assistance of external forces like gravity. It happens because of two key molecular interactions: cohesion and adhesion. Cohesion is the attraction between molecules of the same substance—water molecules are strongly attracted to each other due to hydrogen bonding. Adhesion is the attraction between molecules of different substances—water molecules are also attracted to the walls of a container or the fibers of a paper towel. When adhesion is stronger than cohesion, the liquid tends to spread and climb along the surface. In a narrow tube (a capillary), the combination of these forces, along with surface tension, creates a meniscus and pulls the liquid upward. The narrower the tube, the higher the liquid climbs—a relationship described by the Jurin's law. This is why water rises in a thin glass tube but not in a wide beaker.

To visualize this, think of a group of people holding hands. If they are all attracted to each other (cohesion), they stay together. But if they are also attracted to a wall (adhesion), they might start climbing up the wall, pulling each other along. That's essentially what water molecules do when they encounter a narrow space.

Experiment 1: Walking Water—A Colorful Capillary Show

One of the most visually striking demonstrations of capillary action is the "walking water" experiment. It requires just a few household items: six clear glasses or jars, water, food coloring (red, yellow, and blue), and paper towels. Here's how to set it up:

  • Arrange the glasses in a row. Fill the first, third, and fifth glasses about three-quarters full with water.
  • Add a few drops of red food coloring to the first glass, yellow to the third, and blue to the fifth. Stir each.
  • Fold three paper towels lengthwise into strips. Place one strip so that one end goes into the first glass and the other end into the second (empty) glass. Do the same between the second and third, third and fourth, fourth and fifth, and fifth and sixth glasses.
  • Wait and observe. Within minutes, you'll see the colored water begin to climb the paper towels and drip into the empty glasses. Over the next hour, the colors will mix, creating new hues—orange, green, and purple—in the middle glasses.

This experiment beautifully demonstrates capillary action because the paper towel is made of tiny fibers with narrow spaces between them. The water molecules adhere to the cellulose fibers and pull each other along, defying gravity. The result is a mesmerizing display of color mixing that also teaches about primary and secondary colors. For an extra challenge, try using different types of paper—newspaper, tissue, or cardboard—and compare how fast the water travels. You'll notice that the rate depends on the size of the pores and the absorbency of the material.

Experiment 2: Celery and Food Coloring—A Plant's Drinking Straw

Capillary action isn't just a lab curiosity; it's how plants drink! To see this in action, grab a stalk of celery with leaves, a glass of water, and some red or blue food coloring. Trim the bottom of the celery stalk to create a fresh cut, then place it in the colored water. Over the next few hours, the colored water will travel up the celery through tiny tubes called xylem. These xylem vessels are essentially capillaries that run from the roots to the leaves. As water evaporates from the leaves (a process called transpiration), it creates a negative pressure that pulls more water up—a combination of capillary action and transpirational pull.

After about 4-6 hours, you'll see the celery leaves tinged with color, and if you slice the stalk crosswise, you'll see tiny colored dots—the xylem vessels filled with dye. This experiment is a powerful way to connect the invisible process of capillary action to the visible growth of plants. For a variation, use white carnations or daisies. The petals will change color as the dye reaches them, creating a beautiful, living science project. You can even split the stem of a carnation lengthwise and place each half in a different color to create a two-tone flower—a dramatic demonstration of how water travels through separate pathways.

Experiment 3: Paper Chromatography—Separating Colors with Capillary Action

Capillary action is also the engine behind paper chromatography, a technique used by scientists to separate mixtures. You can try a simple version at home with coffee filters, washable markers, and water. Draw a dot of black marker near the bottom of a coffee filter strip, then hang the strip so that the bottom just touches a shallow layer of water. As the water climbs up the paper via capillary action, it carries the ink with it. But different pigments in the ink have different affinities for the paper and the water, so they travel at different speeds. The result is a rainbow of separated colors—black ink often reveals blues, purples, pinks, and yellows.

This experiment is not only visually appealing but also introduces the concept of chromatography, which is used in forensics, environmental testing, and biochemistry. To extend the activity, try different brands of markers, different solvents (like rubbing alcohol or vinegar), and different types of paper. You'll discover that the separation pattern changes based on the solvent and the paper's absorbency. This is a perfect way to practice the scientific method: form a hypothesis, test it, and analyze the results. For older students, you can measure the Rf value (the ratio of the distance traveled by the pigment to the distance traveled by the solvent) and compare it across different inks.

The Science Behind the Climb: Cohesion, Adhesion, and Surface Tension

To truly appreciate capillary action, we need to zoom in on the molecular level. Water molecules are polar, meaning they have a slight positive charge on one end and a slight negative charge on the other. This polarity allows them to form hydrogen bonds with each other (cohesion) and with other polar surfaces (adhesion). When water meets a narrow tube, the adhesive forces pull the water molecules up the walls, creating a curved meniscus. The cohesive forces then pull the rest of the water along, like a chain of people holding hands and being pulled upward. Surface tension, which is the result of cohesive forces at the surface, also plays a role—it helps maintain the integrity of the water column.

The height to which water rises in a capillary tube is inversely proportional to the tube's radius. This is why plants have extremely narrow xylem vessels—some are only 10 micrometers in diameter. In such tiny tubes, capillary action can lift water several centimeters, but for tall trees, transpiration (evaporation from leaves) provides the additional pull needed to overcome gravity. Together, these forces work in harmony to move water from the soil to the highest branches, a feat that has fascinated scientists for centuries.

You can test the effect of tube diameter at home using glass tubes of different sizes or even straws. Fill a shallow dish with water, place a thin straw and a thick straw upright in the water, and observe the water levels. You'll see that the water climbs higher in the thinner straw. This simple observation is a direct demonstration of Jurin's law and a great way to reinforce the concept.

Real-World Applications: From Ink to Blood

Capillary action is not just a classroom phenomenon; it's at work all around us. In addition to plants, it's how ink flows from a pen to paper, how a paper towel absorbs spills, and how a sponge soaks up water. In the medical field, capillary action is used in test strips for blood glucose monitoring—the blood is drawn into the strip through tiny channels. In construction, capillary action can cause moisture to seep into brick walls, leading to dampness. Engineers and architects must account for this when designing buildings, using damp-proof courses to block the upward movement of water.

Even in the natural world, capillary action helps distribute water in soil, making it available to plant roots. It also plays a role in the movement of groundwater through porous rocks. And in the human body, capillary action assists in the circulation of fluids in the tiny blood vessels called capillaries—which are named after this very phenomenon! Understanding capillary action gives us insight into everything from the design of microfluidic devices to the behavior of water in the environment.

Conclusion

Capillary action is one of those quiet, everyday forces that we often overlook, yet it is essential to life as we know it. Through simple experiments like walking water, celery dye, and paper chromatography, we can make this invisible force visible and tangible. These activities not only teach the principles of cohesion, adhesion, and surface tension but also spark curiosity about how the natural world works. So the next time you see a paper towel soak up a spill or a flower drink from a vase, take a moment to appreciate the incredible journey of water molecules—climbing against gravity, one tiny step at a time. Science is all around us, and with a few household items, you can unlock its secrets. Happy experimenting!
