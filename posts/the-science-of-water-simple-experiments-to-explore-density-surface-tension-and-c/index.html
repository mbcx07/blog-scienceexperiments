The Science of Water: Simple Experiments to Explore Density, Surface Tension, and Capillary Action

2026-05-25 · STEM · Tags: scienceexperiments, stem

Water is the most common substance on Earth, yet it holds some of the most surprising secrets in science. It defies gravity, climbs through tiny spaces, and even forms a stretchy "skin" on its surface. For young scientists, water is a perfect playground—cheap, safe, and endlessly fascinating. In this article, we'll dive into three of water's most intriguing properties: density, surface tension, and capillary action. You'll learn how to demonstrate each one with simple household items, and you'll discover how these concepts connect to real-world phenomena like ocean currents, insect walking on water, and how plants drink. So grab a glass of water, roll up your sleeves, and let's get started!

Density: Why Some Things Float and Others Sink

Density is a measure of how much mass is packed into a given volume. When you drop a rock into a pond, it sinks because the rock is denser than water. A piece of wood floats because it's less dense. But density isn't just about solid objects—liquids have density too, and they can stack on top of each other in fascinating ways. One of the most visually striking experiments to demonstrate this is the rainbow density tower.

To create a density tower, you'll need honey, light corn syrup, dish soap, water (colored with food dye), vegetable oil, and rubbing alcohol. Carefully pour each liquid into a tall glass, starting with the densest (honey) and working your way up to the least dense (rubbing alcohol). The liquids will form distinct layers because they don't mix and have different densities. You can then drop small objects like a grape, a cork, a plastic bead, and a paperclip into the tower and watch them settle at different levels, each resting at the point where their density matches the surrounding liquid.

This experiment is more than just a pretty display. It teaches kids that density is a physical property that determines how substances interact. You can extend the learning by asking questions: Why does the grape sink through the oil but float on the water? What would happen if you added salt to the water layer? (Salt increases water's density, so the grape might float higher.) This hands-on exploration builds intuition about a concept that is central to physics, oceanography, and even meteorology.

Surface Tension: The Skin of Water

Have you ever seen a water strider skimming across a pond without sinking? That's surface tension at work. Water molecules are attracted to each other, and at the surface, they pull together tightly, creating a kind of elastic film. This "skin" can support light objects, but it's easily broken by soap. A classic experiment to visualize surface tension is the pepper-and-soap trick.

Fill a shallow dish with water and sprinkle black pepper evenly across the surface. The pepper flakes will float, resting on the water's surface tension. Now, dip a toothpick into a drop of dish soap and touch it to the center of the water. Instantly, the pepper will scatter to the edges as the soap breaks the surface tension in the middle, pulling the water outward. This dramatic reaction is a great way to show how detergents work—they reduce surface tension, which is why soap helps remove grease and dirt.

Another fun surface tension experiment is the "floating paperclip." Carefully place a dry paperclip on a piece of tissue paper and lower it onto the water's surface. The tissue will sink, but the paperclip will float, held up by the water's surface tension. If you add a drop of soap, the paperclip will sink immediately. Kids love this trick, and it sparks questions about why some insects can walk on water and why soap is so effective at cleaning. You can also explore how temperature affects surface tension—warm water has lower surface tension than cold water, so the pepper experiment works differently with warm water.

Capillary Action: Water's Climbing Power

Capillary action is the ability of water to move through narrow spaces against gravity. It happens because water molecules are attracted to the walls of a tube (adhesion) and to each other (cohesion). This is how plants draw water from their roots to their leaves, and how paper towels soak up spills. A simple and colorful way to demonstrate capillary action is the walking water experiment.

You'll need six clear glasses or jars, water, food coloring (red, yellow, blue), and paper towels. Arrange the glasses in a row, filling every other glass with water and adding a different color to each. Fold paper towels into strips and place one end in a colored water glass and the other end in an empty glass next to it. Over the next few hours, the water will travel up the paper towel and into the empty glass, eventually mixing colors. You'll see the empty glasses fill with a blend of the two adjacent colors, creating a beautiful rainbow effect.

This experiment is perfect for teaching kids about the movement of water in nature. You can ask them to predict what will happen, then observe the process over time. To go deeper, try using different materials—paper towels, napkins, coffee filters, or even celery stalks. Cut a celery stalk in half lengthwise and place it in colored water; within a few hours, the leaves will show the color, demonstrating how plants transport water. This hands-on activity connects directly to biology and environmental science, showing how water moves through soil, plants, and even our own bodies.

Bringing It All Together: A Multi-Layered Experiment

Once your young scientists have explored density, surface tension, and capillary action separately, it's time to combine them into a single, impressive experiment. This not only reinforces the concepts but also encourages critical thinking and problem-solving. One great way to do this is by creating a "water xylophone" that uses all three properties.

Start with several identical glass bottles or jars. Fill them with different amounts of water—from empty to nearly full. When you tap each bottle with a metal spoon, you'll hear different pitches. The bottle with more water produces a lower pitch because the water adds mass and affects the vibration of the glass. This demonstrates density and how it affects sound. But you can also use surface tension and capillary action to change the sound: add a drop of soap to one bottle and see if the pitch changes (it might, due to altered surface tension affecting the vibration). Or, place a paper towel strip connecting two bottles with different water levels and watch the water move, changing the pitch over time.

This integrated experiment is a fantastic way to show that science isn't just isolated facts—it's a web of interconnected principles. Encourage kids to make predictions, record observations, and ask "what if" questions. For example, what happens if you use salt water instead of fresh? What if you use oil instead of water? These open-ended explorations turn a simple activity into a rich learning experience that builds scientific reasoning skills.

Safety and Tips for Success

All the experiments in this article are safe for children, but a few precautions will ensure a smooth experience. Always use food coloring that is non-toxic, and supervise younger children to prevent spills or ingestion of small objects like paperclips. When working with liquids like honey or rubbing alcohol, make sure they are kept away from open flames and that kids don't taste them. For the density tower, use a clear, tall glass and pour the liquids slowly down a spoon to avoid mixing.

To get the best results, use clean, dry containers and fresh materials. For the walking water experiment, use paper towels that are not too thick—they need to absorb water well. If the colors don't mix as expected, check that the paper towels are touching the water and that the glasses are level. Patience is key; some experiments take time, and that's part of the learning process. Encourage kids to keep a science journal where they record their predictions, observations, and conclusions. This turns a fun activity into a structured scientific investigation.

Finally, don't be afraid to let experiments "fail." If the paperclip sinks or the density tower layers mix, that's an opportunity to ask why and try again. Science is about curiosity and resilience, not perfection. Celebrate the questions as much as the answers, and you'll nurture a lifelong love of discovery.

Conclusion

Water is a remarkable teacher. Through simple experiments with density, surface tension, and capillary action, kids can grasp fundamental scientific principles while having a blast. These activities require minimal materials, are easy to set up, and offer endless variations for deeper exploration. Whether you're a parent looking for a rainy-day project or a teacher planning a STEM lesson, these water-based experiments are sure to make a splash. So next time you turn on the tap, remember: you're not just getting water—you're getting a whole laboratory of wonder. Dive in and see what you discover!
