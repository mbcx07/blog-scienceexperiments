The Magic of Magnetism: Hands-On Experiments That Spark Wonder

2026-06-12 · Kids Science · Tags: The, kids science

Magnetism is one of those forces that feels almost like magic. You can’t see it, yet it can push and pull objects across a table, make a compass needle spin, or even levitate a train. For kids, magnets are endlessly fascinating—they’re the perfect gateway into the world of physics. As a science teacher, I’ve seen the spark of curiosity ignite when a child first discovers that a simple bar magnet can attract a paperclip through a piece of paper. That wonder is the foundation of real scientific thinking. In this article, I’ll share a collection of hands-on magnetism experiments that are easy to set up at home or in the classroom, and each one is packed with opportunities to ask questions, make predictions, and learn the underlying science. So grab a few magnets, some household items, and let’s dive into the invisible force that shapes our world.

The Invisible Force: What Is Magnetism?

Before we start experimenting, it helps to understand what magnetism actually is. At its core, magnetism is a force produced by moving electric charges. Every electron in an atom acts like a tiny magnet, but in most materials, these tiny magnets point in random directions, canceling each other out. In ferromagnetic materials like iron, cobalt, and nickel, the electrons can align in the same direction, creating a net magnetic field. That’s why a magnet can attract a paperclip but not a piece of aluminum foil.

Every magnet has two poles: north and south. Opposite poles attract, and like poles repel. This simple rule is the key to many of the experiments we’ll do. But magnetism isn’t just about attraction—it’s also about fields. A magnetic field is the area around a magnet where its force is felt. You can’t see it, but you can map it with iron filings or a compass. Understanding fields is crucial for everything from building electric motors to understanding how the Earth’s magnetic field protects us from solar radiation.

For kids, the best way to grasp these concepts is through direct experience. So let’s get our hands dirty (or rather, our hands full of iron filings) and explore.

Exploring Magnetic Fields with Iron Filings

One of the most visually stunning experiments is mapping a magnetic field using iron filings. You’ll need a strong bar magnet (or a horseshoe magnet), a sheet of white paper, and a small container of iron filings (available online or at science supply stores). Place the magnet under the paper, then gently sprinkle the iron filings over the top. Tap the paper lightly, and watch as the filings arrange themselves into curved lines that trace the magnetic field lines from the north pole to the south pole.

This experiment is a fantastic way to show that the magnetic field is three-dimensional and extends beyond the magnet itself. Kids can try different magnet shapes—bar, disc, ring—and see how the field patterns change. They can also place two magnets with like poles facing each other and observe how the field lines repel, creating a gap between them. Or place opposite poles facing each other and see the lines connect smoothly.

To make it even more engaging, have kids draw the patterns they see in a science notebook. Ask them: Why do the filings form lines? What do the lines tell us about the strength of the field? The lines are closer together near the poles, indicating a stronger field. This is a perfect opportunity to introduce the concept of field strength and how it decreases with distance.

If you don’t have iron filings, you can use a compass to trace the field lines. Place a bar magnet on a piece of paper, then move a small compass around it, marking the direction of the needle at various points. Connect the marks to reveal the field lines. This method is slower but teaches kids how a compass works—which leads us to our next experiment.

The Compass: Finding North with a Needle

Did you know you can make a working compass with just a sewing needle, a piece of cork, and a bowl of water? This classic experiment never fails to amaze. First, magnetize the needle by rubbing it with a strong magnet in one direction about 30 times. Then, push the needle through a small piece of cork so it floats horizontally on the water. The needle will slowly rotate and align itself with the Earth’s magnetic field, pointing roughly north-south.

Why does this work? The Earth itself acts like a giant magnet, with its magnetic south pole near the geographic north pole. The magnetized needle becomes a tiny magnet, and its north pole is attracted to the Earth’s magnetic south pole, which is why it points north. This is a brilliant way to introduce the concept of the Earth’s magnetic field and how navigation has worked for centuries.

Kids can test their compass by turning the bowl and watching the needle return to the same direction. They can also compare it to a real compass to see how accurate it is. To extend the activity, have them try different materials—a paperclip, a piece of wire—and see which ones can be magnetized. This leads to a discussion about ferromagnetic materials and why some metals are magnetic while others aren’t.

Another variation is to make a hanging compass. Tie a string to the middle of a magnetized needle and suspend it from a support. It will also align north-south. This is a great way to show that the magnetic force works even without water.

Building an Electromagnet: The Power of Current

Now it’s time to combine magnetism with electricity. An electromagnet is a magnet that only works when an electric current flows through a coil of wire. This is the principle behind everything from junkyard cranes to MRI machines. To build one, you’ll need a large iron nail (or a bolt), a length of insulated copper wire (about 1-2 meters), a D-cell battery, and some paperclips or small metal objects.

Wrap the wire tightly around the nail, leaving about 15 cm of wire free at each end. Strip the ends of the wire to expose the copper, then attach one end to the positive terminal of the battery and the other to the negative terminal. (You can use tape or alligator clips.) Now bring the nail near the paperclips—they should jump to it! When you disconnect the battery, the paperclips fall off. This is a dramatic demonstration of how electricity creates magnetism.

Kids can experiment with the number of coils. Does more wire mean a stronger magnet? What if you use a thicker nail? What if you use two batteries? Each change is a chance to form a hypothesis and test it. For example, they might predict that more coils will pick up more paperclips. They can count the paperclips as a measure of strength and record their results in a table.

This experiment also introduces the concept of circuits. The battery, wire, and nail form a complete circuit, and the current flows through the wire, creating a magnetic field around the nail. When you disconnect the wire, the circuit breaks, and the magnetism disappears. This is a perfect segue into discussing how electric motors and generators work, and why electromagnets are so useful because they can be turned on and off.

Magnetic Slime: A Gooey Lesson in Ferrofluids

If you want to combine chemistry and physics, magnetic slime is the way to go. This is a more advanced experiment, but it’s incredibly rewarding. You’ll need liquid starch, white school glue, and iron oxide powder (available online). Mix equal parts glue and liquid starch, then add a spoonful of iron oxide powder and knead until the slime forms. The result is a stretchy, gooey substance that responds to a strong magnet.

When you bring a neodymium magnet near the slime, it will ooze and stretch toward the magnet, almost like a living creature. This happens because the iron oxide particles are suspended in the slime, and when a magnetic field is applied, they align and pull the slime along. This is a simplified version of a ferrofluid, which is a liquid that becomes strongly magnetized in the presence of a magnetic field.

Kids can explore how the slime behaves differently with different magnets. A weak magnet might only cause a slight bulge, while a strong magnet can make the slime climb up the side of the container. They can also try to shape the slime into a ball and see how it flattens when the magnet is placed underneath. This experiment is a fantastic way to discuss the concept of magnetic susceptibility and how materials can be classified as ferromagnetic, paramagnetic, or diamagnetic.

Safety note: Iron oxide powder can be messy, so do this on a tray or in a shallow container. Also, keep magnets away from electronic devices and credit cards, as they can erase data.

The Science Behind the Fun: Real-World Applications

After all these experiments, kids will be buzzing with questions. That’s the perfect time to connect the dots to real-world technology. Magnets are everywhere—in speakers, hard drives, credit card strips, and even in the trains that use magnetic levitation to float above the tracks. The Earth’s magnetic field guides migratory birds and sea turtles, and it protects us from harmful solar particles.

One of the most exciting applications is in medicine. MRI machines use powerful magnets to create detailed images of the inside of the body. The magnets align the protons in your body, and when they relax, they emit signals that a computer turns into a picture. This is a great way to show kids that the simple principles they’ve been playing with have life-saving implications.

You can also discuss how electromagnets are used in recycling plants to separate steel from other materials, and in cranes to lift heavy scrap metal. The ability to turn magnetism on and off is what makes these applications possible. Encourage kids to think of other uses—maybe they can invent something new!

To wrap up, have kids reflect on what they’ve learned. Ask them: What surprised you the most? What do you want to explore next? This kind of reflection is a key part of the scientific process. It turns a fun activity into a genuine learning experience.

Conclusion

Magnetism is a gateway to understanding the invisible forces that shape our universe. Through these hands-on experiments, kids not only learn the science behind magnets but also develop critical thinking skills—making predictions, testing hypotheses, and drawing conclusions. Whether they’re mapping magnetic fields with iron filings, building a compass, or creating an electromagnet, they’re doing real science. And the best part? They’re having fun while doing it. So the next time you see a magnet, don’t just stick it to the fridge—take a moment to wonder at the invisible force that makes it work. Then, grab some supplies and start experimenting. The world of magnetism is waiting to be discovered.
