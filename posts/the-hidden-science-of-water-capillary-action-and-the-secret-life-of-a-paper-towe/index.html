The Hidden Science of Water: Capillary Action and the Secret Life of a Paper Towel

2026-08-14 · Kids Science · Tags: The, scienceexperiments, kids science

Water is everywhere. We drink it, wash with it, and watch it fall from the sky. But for all its familiarity, water is a master of disguise, hiding some of its most fascinating talents in plain sight. One of the most captivating, yet often overlooked, phenomena is capillary action—the ability of water to defy gravity and climb up narrow spaces. It’s the force that carries moisture from the roots of a giant redwood to its highest leaves, and it’s the same force that makes a simple paper towel an incredible scientific instrument. In this article, we’ll dive deep into the secret life of a paper towel, exploring how capillary action works, why it matters, and how you can turn this everyday object into a springboard for a week’s worth of engaging science experiments with your kids. Forget the textbook diagrams; we’re going to get our hands wet and watch science happen in real time.

The Invisible Force: What Exactly Is Capillary Action?

Before we start our experiments, it’s essential to understand the invisible cast of characters that makes capillary action possible. The stars of the show are two related but distinct forces: cohesion and adhesion. Cohesion is the attractive force between molecules of the same substance. Water molecules are incredibly social, clinging to each other through hydrogen bonds. This is why water forms droplets on a freshly waxed car—the water molecules are more attracted to each other than to the wax. Adhesion, on the other hand, is the attractive force between molecules of different substances. Water is also a great diplomat, readily forming bonds with other materials like glass, wood, and yes, the cellulose fibers in a paper towel.

Capillary action is what happens when adhesion and cohesion team up against gravity. When you place the edge of a paper towel in a glass of water, the water molecules are strongly attracted to the cellulose fibers (adhesion). This attraction pulls the water molecules upward into the tiny spaces between the fibers. As the first water molecules climb, they pull their neighbors along with them (cohesion), creating a chain of water molecules that continues to move upward. The narrower the space (or capillary), the higher the water can climb because the adhesive force is concentrated on a smaller amount of water. This is a perfect, tangible example of a concept that often feels abstract. When your child asks, "Why is the paper towel wet all the way up?" you can confidently explain that they are watching a microscopic tug-of-war between gravity and the water's social nature.

Experiment 1: The Classic Rainbow Walk

Now that we have the science down, let’s put it into action. The "Rainbow Walk" is a classic, visually stunning experiment that demonstrates capillary action in a way that’s impossible to ignore. It’s perfect for a rainy afternoon or a quick after-school activity. Here’s what you’ll need:

  • Six clear glasses or jars
  • Water
  • Food coloring (red, yellow, and blue)
  • Paper towels

Start by arranging your glasses in a circle or a straight line. Fill every other glass about three-quarters full with water. Leave the empty glasses between the water-filled ones. Add a few drops of red food coloring to the first water glass, yellow to the second, and blue to the third. Next, fold a paper towel in half lengthwise, and then in half again, to create a long, narrow strip. Do this for three strips total. Place one end of a strip into the red water glass and the other end into the adjacent empty glass. Repeat this process, connecting each water glass to an empty glass with a new paper towel strip. The magic begins immediately.

Within a few minutes, you’ll see the colored water begin to creep up the paper towels. Over the next hour or so, the water will travel along the entire strip and start dripping into the empty glasses. But here’s the real payoff: the empty glasses will begin to fill with a mixture of colors. The red and yellow water will mix to create orange, the yellow and blue will create green, and the blue and red will create purple. This experiment is a beautiful visual lesson in both capillary action and color theory. It’s a wonderful opportunity to discuss primary and secondary colors while also reinforcing the idea that water is a hard worker, even when it’s just sitting still.

Experiment 2: The Walking Water Garden

If the Rainbow Walk is the classic, then the Walking Water Garden is its more ambitious, artistic cousin. This variation takes the same core principle and turns it into a longer-term project that can last for days, making it an excellent choice for a classroom setting or a weekend-long home investigation. For this experiment, you’ll want to create a more complex network of paper towel bridges. Instead of a simple line, arrange your glasses in a circle, filling every glass with water and a different color of food coloring. Then, create a web of paper towel strips, connecting each glass to its neighbors.

As the water walks, it will not only mix colors but also create a stunning display of liquid engineering. The key here is to observe and document. Have your kids take a photo every hour and compare the progression. You’ll notice that the water doesn’t all travel at the same speed. The paper towels that are more loosely folded will allow water to move faster than tightly rolled ones. This is a fantastic opportunity to introduce the concept of variables. Ask questions like, "What happens if we use a thicker paper towel?" or "What happens if we use a different material, like a napkin or a piece of cloth?" Let your kids hypothesize, test, and observe. This turns a simple craft into a genuine scientific inquiry, fostering the kind of critical thinking that is far more valuable than just getting the "right" answer.

Experiment 3: The Capillary Rainbow in a Glass

This next experiment is a personal favorite because it’s fast, dramatic, and requires almost no setup. It’s the perfect "wow" moment to kick off a science lesson. You’ll need a single glass, water, a paper towel, and a few different colors of washable markers. Cut a strip of paper towel about two inches wide and eight inches long. About an inch from the bottom of the strip, draw a thick line of dots using your markers, making a small rainbow of colors. Make sure the dots are close together but not overlapping.

Now, pour about half an inch of water into the bottom of your glass. Tape the top of the paper towel strip to the outside of the glass so that the bottom edge (with the marker dots) just barely touches the water. Now, watch closely. The water will immediately begin to climb up the paper towel. As it reaches the marker dots, it will dissolve the pigments and carry them up the strip. But here’s the beautiful part: the different colors are made of different molecules that have different levels of solubility and different molecular weights. This means they will travel at different speeds, causing the colors to separate and spread out into a stunning, tie-dye-like pattern. This simple activity is a perfect introduction to chromatography, the technique scientists use to separate mixtures. It’s a beautiful metaphor for how science can reveal hidden complexities in things that seem simple, like a black marker line, which is actually a secret rainbow.

Going Deeper: The Science of Plant Stems

Once you’ve mastered the paper towel, it’s time to take your capillary action exploration into the great outdoors. The most powerful demonstration of this force is found in nature. For this experiment, you’ll need a few fresh, white flowers (carnations or daisies work best), a few glasses, water, and food coloring. Trim the stems of the flowers at an angle and place each one in a glass of colored water. Then, wait. Over the next 12 to 24 hours, you’ll see the petals of the white flowers begin to change color, tinted with the same hue as the water they’re drinking.

This is a stunning visual that connects the abstract concept of capillary action to the very real, life-sustaining process of how plants drink. The water doesn't just magically appear in the petals; it is drawn up through tiny tubes in the stem called xylem. These xylem are nature’s capillaries. You can even take this experiment a step further by splitting the stem of a single flower lengthwise and placing each half in a different color of water. This will result in a beautiful, two-tone flower, demonstrating that the water travels in distinct channels. This is a powerful lesson that the same science we see in a paper towel is the very same science that keeps our planet's ecosystems alive. It helps children see that science isn't just a set of classroom rules; it's the operating system of the natural world.

Conclusion: The Unseen World, Right at Your Fingertips

The next time you reach for a paper towel to clean up a spill, take a moment to appreciate the incredible physics at play. That simple, disposable sheet is a sophisticated network of microscopic channels, perfectly designed to exploit the cohesive and adhesive properties of water. Through these simple experiments, we’ve seen how a basic household item can become a portal to understanding fundamental scientific principles. We’ve watched color walk, gardens grow, and hidden rainbows emerge, all from the power of capillary action. The goal of science education isn’t to fill a child’s head with facts, but to open their eyes to the extraordinary world that exists right under their noses. So, gather your supplies, get a little messy, and let the water walk. You might just find that the most profound scientific discoveries are hiding in your kitchen cabinet, waiting for a curious mind to come along and ask, "Why?"
