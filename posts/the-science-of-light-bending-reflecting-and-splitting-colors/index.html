The Science of Light: Bending, Reflecting, and Splitting Colors

2026-08-11 · Kids Science · Tags: kids science, scienceexperiments, The

Light is the silent architect of our world. It paints the sky at dawn, dances through leaves, and carries the colors of a rainbow. Yet for most children, light is simply something that exists—until you show them it can be bent, bounced, and broken into a spectrum of hues. As a science teacher, I've found that light experiments are among the most magical and accessible ways to spark curiosity. They require little more than household items, yet they reveal the fundamental physics that govern everything from eyeglasses to fiber-optic internet. In this article, we'll dive into hands-on activities that let young scientists explore refraction, reflection, dispersion, and color mixing—all while having fun and building a deeper understanding of the invisible forces that shape our visual world.

The Nature of Light: More Than Meets the Eye

Before we start bending beams, it helps to understand what light actually is. Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation that travels in waves. These waves have different lengths, and our eyes interpret those lengths as colors. Red has the longest wavelength we can see, while violet has the shortest. When light travels through a vacuum, it moves at a constant speed of about 299,792 kilometers per second. But when light enters a different medium—like water, glass, or even air of varying temperature—it slows down and changes direction. This bending is called refraction, and it's the secret behind lenses, prisms, and the shimmering mirage on a hot road.

Reflection, on the other hand, is when light bounces off a surface. A mirror reflects almost all light, while a white wall reflects most but scatters it. The law of reflection states that the angle at which light hits a surface equals the angle at which it bounces off. This simple rule explains why you can see yourself in a still pond but not in a rough one. Dispersion is the process of splitting white light into its component colors, which happens because different wavelengths slow down by different amounts in a medium. That's why a prism creates a rainbow. These three phenomena—refraction, reflection, and dispersion—are the stars of our experiments.

Bending Light: The Disappearing Pencil Trick

One of the simplest and most dramatic demonstrations of refraction uses a glass of water and a pencil. Fill a clear glass about three-quarters full with water. Place a pencil or a straw inside, leaning it against the side. Look at the glass from the side—the pencil appears broken or bent at the waterline. This happens because light from the submerged part of the pencil travels through water, then glass, then air, bending each time it crosses a boundary. Your brain assumes light travels in straight lines, so it interprets the bent path as a physical bend in the pencil.

To take it further, try placing a coin in an empty bowl. Position your eye so the coin is just out of sight behind the rim. Now slowly pour water into the bowl without moving your head. The coin will appear to rise into view! This is because the water refracts light from the coin, bending it over the rim and into your eye. This experiment is a fantastic way to show that light doesn't always travel in straight lines when it passes through different materials. For older kids, you can measure the angle of refraction using a protractor and a laser pointer (with adult supervision) to see how the angle changes with different liquids like oil or saltwater.

Reflecting Light: Build Your Own Periscope

Reflection is all about mirrors, and there's no more engaging way to explore it than by building a periscope. A periscope lets you see over walls or around corners by using two mirrors set at 45-degree angles. You'll need a long cardboard tube (like from wrapping paper), two small flat mirrors, and some tape or glue. Cut two square holes at opposite ends of the tube, each on a different side. Attach the mirrors inside the tube at 45-degree angles, facing the holes. When you look through one hole, light enters the other, reflects off the first mirror, travels down the tube, reflects off the second mirror, and reaches your eye. It's a classic example of how reflection can redirect light.

For a simpler version, try the classic mirror maze. Place two mirrors facing each other and put a small object between them. You'll see infinite reflections, each one slightly dimmer due to light loss. This happens because each reflection bounces light back and forth, and some light is absorbed by the mirror each time. You can also explore how the angle between two mirrors changes the number of images. Place two mirrors at 90 degrees and look at your face—you'll see three images. At 60 degrees, you'll see five. The formula is 360 divided by the angle minus one, but kids can just observe the pattern. This hands-on exploration of reflection builds intuition about how light travels and how we see the world.

Splitting Light: Create a Rainbow with a Prism or a CD

Isaac Newton famously used a glass prism to split white light into a spectrum of colors. You can replicate this with a simple CD or a glass of water and a mirror. For the CD method, place a CD on a flat surface with the shiny side up. Shine a flashlight or a smartphone light at an angle onto the CD. You'll see a rainbow reflected onto a nearby wall or piece of paper. The CD acts as a diffraction grating, which separates light into its component wavelengths. The grooves on the CD are spaced similarly to the wavelengths of visible light, causing different colors to interfere and spread out.

For a more traditional prism effect, fill a clear glass with water and place a small mirror inside at an angle. Shine a flashlight through the water onto the mirror, and adjust the angle until you see a rainbow on the wall. The water and mirror together act as a prism, refracting and dispersing the light. This experiment is perfect for a sunny day—use sunlight for the brightest results. As you watch the colors appear, explain that white light is actually a mixture of all colors. Each color has a different wavelength, and when light passes through a medium, each wavelength bends by a slightly different amount. Red bends the least, violet the most, so they spread out into a band. This is the same physics that creates rainbows in the sky after a storm.

Mixing Colors: Additive and Subtractive Magic

Once you've split light into colors, it's time to put them back together. This is where color mixing comes in. There are two ways to mix colors: additive and subtractive. Additive mixing happens with light itself. If you shine red, green, and blue lights onto the same spot, they combine to create white light. You can demonstrate this with three flashlights and colored cellophane or filters. Cover each flashlight with a different color (red, green, blue) and shine them onto a white wall. Overlap the beams—you'll see yellow (red+green), cyan (green+blue), magenta (red+blue), and white (all three). This is how your TV and computer screens work, using tiny red, green, and blue pixels.

Subtractive mixing involves pigments, like paint or markers. When you mix paints, you're subtracting colors because each pigment absorbs certain wavelengths and reflects others. For example, mixing yellow and blue paint gives green because yellow absorbs blue light, blue absorbs red light, and together they reflect only green. A fun activity is to make a color wheel using a spinning top or a paper plate. Color a paper plate with the seven rainbow colors, then spin it fast. The colors will appear to blend into white or gray, depending on the balance. This happens because your eye's persistence of vision merges the rapid succession of colors. It's a great way to show that white light is a combination of all colors, and that our perception of color is a complex interaction between light, objects, and our brains.

Light and Shadows: The Play of Angles and Distance

Shadows are the flip side of light—they're what happens when an object blocks light. But shadows aren't just dark blobs; they're rich with information about the light source and the object. To explore this, take a flashlight and a variety of objects (a toy, a book, a hand). Shine the light on a wall and hold the object between the light and the wall. Move the object closer to the light—the shadow gets bigger. Move it closer to the wall—the shadow gets smaller and sharper. This is because light travels in straight lines, and the shadow is the projection of the object's outline. The closer the object is to the light source, the more the light rays diverge, creating a larger shadow.

You can also create a sundial to track the sun's movement. Place a stick vertically in the ground or in a lump of clay on a sunny day. Mark the tip of the shadow every hour. You'll notice the shadow changes length and direction as the sun moves across the sky. This is a direct observation of Earth's rotation and the sun's apparent motion. For a more artistic twist, try making shadow puppets with your hands. By changing the distance and angle of your hands relative to the light, you can create different shapes and sizes. This experiment teaches kids about the geometry of light and how shadows can be manipulated. It's also a wonderful way to spend an afternoon outdoors, combining science with creativity.

Conclusion: The Endless Fascination of Light

Light is a gateway to understanding the universe. From the simple act of bending a pencil to the complex physics of a rainbow, these experiments reveal that the world is far more intricate than it appears. By exploring refraction, reflection, dispersion, and color mixing, children not only learn scientific principles but also develop critical thinking and observation skills. They begin to ask questions: Why is the sky blue? How do glasses work? What makes a diamond sparkle? Each experiment is a stepping stone to deeper inquiry. So gather your flashlights, mirrors, and glasses of water, and let the light lead the way. The next time you see a rainbow, you'll know it's not just magic—it's science, and it's yours to explore.
