The Science of Air Pressure: Crushing Cans, Levitating Balls, and Other Invisible Forces

2026-05-16 · STEM · Tags: scienceexperiments, stem

Air is all around us, yet we rarely give it a second thought. It's invisible, odorless, and seemingly weightless—but it exerts a constant force on every surface it touches. At sea level, that force is about 14.7 pounds per square inch, enough to crush a soda can in an instant if the pressure inside is removed. This hidden power is air pressure, and it's responsible for everything from the weather to the way your ears pop on a plane. For young scientists, air pressure offers a treasure trove of dramatic, hands-on experiments that feel like magic but are firmly rooted in physics. In this article, we'll explore the science behind air pressure and walk through several safe, easy-to-replicate experiments that will have your kids (and you) saying, "Whoa!"

Understanding Air Pressure: The Invisible Ocean

Think of the atmosphere as a giant ocean of air, with us living at the bottom. Just as water pressure increases the deeper you dive, air pressure is greatest at the surface because it's supporting the weight of all the air above. This pressure pushes in all directions—up, down, and sideways. It's what keeps our bodies from exploding (our internal pressure balances it) and what makes a suction cup stick to a wall. When you change the amount of air inside a container, you create a pressure difference. If the outside pressure is higher, it pushes inward; if the inside pressure is higher, it pushes outward. This simple principle is the key to many classic experiments.

To get started, you don't need fancy equipment. Most experiments use household items like cans, bottles, balloons, and water. The best part? They're safe, quick, and visually stunning. Let's dive into four experiments that demonstrate air pressure in action.

Experiment 1: The Crushing Can—A Dramatic Demonstration of Vacuum

This is the classic "imploding can" experiment that never fails to amaze. You'll need an empty aluminum soda can, a stove or hot plate, a pair of tongs, a shallow bowl of cold water, and a few drops of water inside the can. Here's the procedure: Place the can on the stove with a small amount of water (about a teaspoon) inside. Heat it until you see steam coming out of the opening. The water boils, and the steam pushes out the air. Using tongs, quickly invert the can and plunge it mouth-down into the cold water. The can will crumple with a satisfying crunch.

What's happening? The steam inside the can is at a high temperature and pressure, but when you cool it suddenly, the steam condenses back into liquid water, which takes up much less space. This creates a partial vacuum inside the can. The outside air pressure, still at 14.7 psi, now has nothing to push against from the inside, so it crushes the can inward. It's a powerful visual of how strong air pressure really is. For younger kids, you can explain it as "the air outside is pushing harder than the air inside."

Safety note: Always supervise children around heat and hot water. Use tongs to handle the hot can, and make sure the bowl of water is shallow to avoid splashing.

Experiment 2: The Levitating Ball—Bernoulli's Principle in Action

This one feels like magic: a ping-pong ball floats in mid-air when you blow air over it. You'll need a hair dryer (or a straw and a friend) and a ping-pong ball. Turn the hair dryer on and point it upward. Place the ball in the stream of air. It will hover, seemingly defying gravity. You can even tilt the dryer slightly and the ball will stay in the stream.

The science behind this is Bernoulli's principle, which states that faster-moving air has lower pressure. As the air from the dryer rushes past the ball, it moves faster than the still air around it. This creates a region of lower pressure above and around the ball. The higher pressure of the surrounding air pushes the ball back into the stream, keeping it centered. The upward force of the moving air also counteracts gravity, so the ball floats. This is the same principle that allows airplanes to fly and curveballs to curve.

To make it a true experiment, try different objects: a crumpled piece of paper, a small balloon, or a foam ball. Which ones float? Which ones don't? Why? You can also use a straw to blow air across the top of a piece of paper—the paper will rise. This is a great way to explore how air pressure changes with speed.

Experiment 3: The Egg in a Bottle—A Classic Pressure Puzzle

This experiment is a crowd-pleaser and a bit of a brain teaser. You'll need a hard-boiled egg (peeled), a glass bottle with a mouth slightly smaller than the egg (like a milk bottle), a piece of paper, and a match or lighter. Light the paper on fire and drop it into the bottle. Quickly place the egg on top of the bottle's mouth. The egg will be sucked into the bottle with a pop.

Here's the science: The burning paper heats the air inside the bottle, causing it to expand. Some of the air escapes. When the flame goes out, the air cools and contracts, creating a lower pressure inside the bottle than outside. The higher outside pressure pushes the egg into the bottle. It's a dramatic demonstration of how temperature affects air pressure.

To get the egg out, you can invert the bottle and blow air into it, or you can heat the bottle again to create pressure that pushes the egg out. This experiment is perfect for discussing the relationship between temperature, volume, and pressure (the ideal gas law, simplified). For safety, always have an adult handle the fire, and use a glass bottle that can withstand heat.

Experiment 4: The Cartesian Diver—A Submarine in a Bottle

This is a more subtle but equally fascinating experiment. You'll need a clear plastic bottle (with a cap), a small glass dropper or a pen cap with a bit of clay, and water. Fill the bottle almost to the top with water. Place the dropper (or pen cap) in the bottle so it floats, with just a tiny bit of air inside. Seal the bottle tightly. Now squeeze the sides of the bottle. The dropper will sink. Release the squeeze, and it will rise. You can control it like a submarine.

The science is all about pressure and density. When you squeeze the bottle, you increase the pressure on the water. That pressure is transmitted to the air pocket inside the dropper, compressing it. The compressed air takes up less space, so water enters the dropper, making it heavier and more dense than the surrounding water—so it sinks. When you release the pressure, the air expands, pushing water out, and the dropper becomes less dense and floats back up. This is exactly how real submarines control their buoyancy.

This experiment is great for older kids because it introduces the concept of compressibility and density in a hands-on way. You can vary the amount of air in the dropper to see how it affects the diving behavior. It's also a wonderful way to talk about how pressure is transmitted through fluids (Pascal's principle).

Bringing It All Together: The Power of Air Pressure

These four experiments are just the tip of the iceberg. Air pressure is at work in so many everyday phenomena—from drinking through a straw to the way a vacuum cleaner works. By doing these hands-on activities, kids not only learn the science but also develop critical thinking skills. They start to ask questions: Why did the can crush? Why does the ball float? What would happen if I used a bigger bottle? That curiosity is the foundation of scientific inquiry.

To extend the learning, encourage kids to design their own variations. For example, can they make a can crush without heat? (Hint: use a vacuum pump.) Can they make a Cartesian diver with a different object? What about testing different liquids? The possibilities are endless. And remember, the best experiments are the ones that go wrong—they lead to the most interesting questions.

Conclusion

Air pressure is an invisible force that shapes our world, and these experiments make it visible and tangible. From the dramatic implosion of a can to the gentle levitation of a ball, each activity reveals a different aspect of this fundamental principle. Whether you're a parent looking for a rainy-day activity or a teacher planning a STEM lesson, these experiments are sure to spark wonder and deepen understanding. So gather your materials, put on your safety goggles, and get ready to feel the pressure—in the best way possible. The invisible has never been so exciting.
