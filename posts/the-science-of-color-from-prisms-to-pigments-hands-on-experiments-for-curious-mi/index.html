The Science of Color: From Prisms to Pigments – Hands-On Experiments for Curious Minds

2026-08-03 · Projects · Tags: projects, The, scienceexperiments

Color is everywhere, yet we rarely stop to think about what it really is. Is it a property of the object itself, or something our brains create? The truth is both—and that makes color one of the most fascinating subjects for hands-on science. When you explore color through experiments, you're not just painting or mixing; you're diving into physics, chemistry, and biology all at once. You'll learn how light bends, how molecules absorb and reflect wavelengths, and how your eyes and brain interpret those signals. Best of all, you can do most of these experiments with items you already have at home. So grab a flashlight, some food coloring, and a few leaves—it's time to turn your kitchen into a rainbow laboratory.

The Physics of Light and Color

Before you start mixing paints, it helps to understand what color actually is. Visible light is a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum, and each color corresponds to a different wavelength. Red has the longest wavelength, violet the shortest. When white light—like sunlight—passes through a prism, it bends (refracts) at different angles depending on wavelength, spreading into a spectrum. This is how rainbows form, and you can recreate it at home with a simple glass of water and a mirror.

Try this: fill a clear glass with water, place a small mirror inside at an angle, and shine a flashlight onto the mirror. Hold a piece of white paper above the glass to catch the reflected light. You'll see a mini rainbow! This works because the water and mirror act like a prism, separating the white light into its component colors. It's a beautiful demonstration that white light is actually a mixture of all colors.

But why do objects appear colored? When light hits an object, some wavelengths are absorbed and others are reflected. A red apple looks red because it reflects red wavelengths and absorbs most others. The color you see is the light that bounces off the object and reaches your eye. This is a key concept: color is not inherent in the object—it's a result of how it interacts with light.

Mixing Colors: The Science of Perception

Now that you know how light works, let's talk about mixing colors. There are two different systems: additive and subtractive. Additive color mixing applies to light. When you shine red, green, and blue lights together, they combine to create white light. This is how your TV and computer screens work. Subtractive mixing applies to pigments—paints, inks, and dyes. When you mix paints, you're subtracting wavelengths because each pigment absorbs certain colors and reflects others. The primary colors for pigments are cyan, magenta, and yellow, but in school we often use red, blue, and yellow as a simplified version.

Here's a fun experiment to explore subtractive mixing: get some watercolor paints or food coloring in red, blue, and yellow. Mix them in different proportions and observe the results. You'll notice that red and blue make purple, blue and yellow make green, and red and yellow make orange. But what happens if you mix all three? You get a muddy brown—because you're absorbing more and more wavelengths, leaving less light to reflect.

To see additive mixing, you can use three flashlights with colored cellophane (red, green, blue). Shine them on a white wall so they overlap. You'll see that red + green = yellow, green + blue = cyan, and red + blue = magenta. All three together make white. This is a great way to show kids that color mixing depends on whether you're dealing with light or pigment—a common source of confusion.

The Chemistry of Color: pH Indicators

Color isn't just about physics—it's also a powerful tool in chemistry. Many natural substances change color depending on the acidity or alkalinity of their environment. These are called pH indicators, and one of the best is red cabbage. The pigment in red cabbage, called anthocyanin, turns red in acidic solutions, purple in neutral ones, and green or yellow in basic (alkaline) solutions.

To make your own indicator, chop a few red cabbage leaves and boil them in water for about 10 minutes. Strain the liquid—you'll have a deep purple solution. Now, set up a series of clear cups with different household liquids: lemon juice (acidic), vinegar (acidic), baking soda dissolved in water (basic), soapy water (basic), and plain water (neutral). Add a few drops of the cabbage juice to each cup and watch the colors change. Lemon juice will turn pink, vinegar will turn red, baking soda water will turn blue or green, and soapy water will turn yellow-green. This is a vivid demonstration of how chemical reactions can be detected by color changes.

You can also use this indicator to test other substances—orange juice, milk, soda, even saliva. It's a fantastic way to introduce the concept of pH and acids/bases to young scientists. And it's completely safe, as long as you don't drink the mixtures (they're not toxic, but they don't taste great).

Paper Chromatography: Unmasking Hidden Colors

Have you ever wondered why a black marker isn't just black? In reality, most black inks are made from a mixture of several different pigments. Paper chromatography is a technique that separates these pigments based on their solubility and how strongly they stick to the paper. It's a simple but powerful experiment that reveals the hidden colors in everyday items.

Here's how to do it: cut a strip of coffee filter or paper towel. Draw a small dot of black marker about 2 cm from the bottom. Place the strip in a glass with a small amount of water, making sure the water level is below the dot. As the water travels up the paper, it carries the pigments with it. Different pigments move at different speeds, so they separate into distinct bands. You'll likely see blues, reds, yellows, and even purples that were invisible in the original dot.

Try this with different brands of markers, or with food coloring, or even with crushed leaves (you'll need to extract the pigments with rubbing alcohol first). Each substance will produce a unique pattern, called a chromatogram. This is the same principle scientists use to analyze complex mixtures, from DNA to pollutants. It's a great way to show kids that science is about uncovering hidden information.

Natural Pigments: Making Your Own Paint

Now that you've seen how colors separate, let's put them back together—but this time, you'll create your own pigments from natural sources. Many plants, spices, and even vegetables contain vibrant pigments that can be turned into paint. This is a wonderful activity that combines art and science, and it teaches kids about the chemistry of color in a tangible way.

Start with turmeric, which contains curcumin, a bright yellow pigment. Mix a teaspoon of turmeric powder with a little water and a few drops of rubbing alcohol to make a paste. You can use this as a paint. For red, try beetroot or pomegranate juice. For green, blend spinach leaves with a little water and strain. For blue, you can use red cabbage juice mixed with a bit of baking soda (it turns green, but you can also try blueberries). Each pigment has its own properties—some are more lightfast, some are more soluble.

To make a more permanent paint, you can mix your pigment with a binder like egg yolk (tempera) or a little flour and water. This is exactly how ancient artists made their paints. You can then paint on paper, fabric, or even wood. This experiment not only teaches about pigments but also about the history of art and the intersection of science and creativity.

Conclusion

Color is a gateway to understanding the world. Through these experiments, you've explored the physics of light, the chemistry of pH, the technique of chromatography, and the art of natural pigments. Each one reveals a different layer of what color really is—a dance of photons, molecules, and perception. The best part is that you can keep experimenting: try new materials, test new variables, and ask new questions. Why does a sunset look red? Why do some colors fade in sunlight? How do animals see color differently? The answers are out there, waiting for you to discover them. So keep your eyes open, your curiosity sharp, and your hands busy—because the science of color is just one of the many wonders you can explore right at home.
