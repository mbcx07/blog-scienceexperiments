The Hidden Science of Water: Experiments That Explore Its Most Surprising Properties

2026-05-22 · Experiments · Tags: experiments, The

Water is the most common substance on Earth, yet it is also one of the strangest. It defies the rules of chemistry, expands when it freezes, and clings to itself in ways that seem almost magical. For students, water is a gateway to understanding some of the most fundamental concepts in science—from molecular bonding to density and surface tension. In this article, we will dive into the hidden science of water through a series of hands-on experiments that are simple to set up, visually engaging, and packed with real learning. Whether you are a teacher looking for a classroom activity or a parent seeking an afternoon project, these experiments will turn an ordinary glass of water into a laboratory of wonder.

The Incredible Shrinking Water: Exploring Cohesion and Adhesion

Before we get to the dramatic experiments, let us start with a quiet one that reveals a lot about how water molecules behave. You will need a glass of water, a paperclip, and a dropper. Fill the glass to the very brim, so the water is level with the rim. Now, ask your students to predict: how many drops of water can you add before it overflows? Most will guess ten or twenty. The answer, surprisingly, is often over fifty.

This experiment demonstrates cohesion—the tendency of water molecules to stick to each other. Because water molecules are polar, they form hydrogen bonds with one another, creating a sort of elastic skin on the surface. As you carefully add drops, the water level rises above the rim, forming a dome. The water holds together because the cohesive forces between molecules are stronger than the pull of gravity trying to spill it over.

To extend this activity, try adding a drop of dish soap to the water after you have built your dome. The soap breaks the hydrogen bonds, and the dome collapses instantly, spilling water everywhere. This is a dramatic and immediate demonstration of how surfactants work—a concept that connects to everything from laundry detergent to the way our lungs function.

The Dancing Raisins: A Lesson in Buoyancy and Gas

If you want an experiment that feels like magic but teaches real physics, the dancing raisins experiment is a classic. Fill a clear glass with carbonated water or clear soda. Drop in a few raisins. At first, they sink to the bottom. Within a minute, they begin to rise, fall, and dance around the glass.

The science behind this is all about buoyancy and gas. Carbonated drinks contain dissolved carbon dioxide gas. The rough, wrinkled surface of a raisin provides nucleation sites where gas bubbles can form. As bubbles accumulate on the raisin, they increase its volume without significantly increasing its weight. This lowers the raisin's density below that of the liquid, so it floats to the surface. When the bubbles pop at the surface, the raisin loses its buoyancy and sinks again, starting the cycle anew.

This experiment is a wonderful way to introduce the concept of density in a way that is visual and dynamic. You can vary the experiment by using different objects—like small pasta shapes or beads—to see which ones dance and which ones do not. Ask students to hypothesize why some objects work better than others. The answer lies in the surface texture and the ability of gas bubbles to cling to the object.

Walking Water: Capillary Action in Action

One of the most visually stunning water experiments is the walking water experiment. You will need six clear glasses or jars, water, food coloring in three colors (red, yellow, blue), and paper towels. Arrange the glasses in a row. Fill the first, third, and fifth glasses about three-quarters full with water. Add red dye to the first, yellow to the third, and blue to the fifth. Leave the second, fourth, and sixth glasses empty. Now, fold paper towels into strips and place one end in a colored glass and the other end in the adjacent empty glass, creating a bridge between all six glasses.

Within a few hours, the water will begin to "walk" up the paper towel and into the empty glasses. This is capillary action, the same force that allows plants to draw water from their roots up to their leaves. Water molecules are adhesive—they stick to the cellulose fibers in the paper towel—and cohesive—they stick to each other. The combination of these forces pulls the water upward against gravity.

As the empty glasses fill, the colors mix. The yellow and blue combine to make green, red and yellow make orange, and so on. This experiment is a fantastic way to teach both capillary action and color theory. It takes time, which makes it perfect for a classroom setting where students can check on the progress throughout the day and record their observations.

The Magic of the Water Cycle in a Bag

Water is always moving, cycling through evaporation, condensation, and precipitation. But it can be hard for students to grasp the scale of the water cycle when they see it happening over weeks and across vast distances. This experiment brings the entire cycle into a small, sealable plastic bag.

To do this, take a quart-sized zip-top bag and draw a simple scene on it with a permanent marker—maybe a sun in the corner and some clouds. Fill the bottom of the bag with about an inch of water and add a few drops of blue food coloring. Seal the bag tightly and tape it to a sunny window. Over the course of a few hours, you will see water droplets form on the inside of the bag, and eventually, they will gather and fall back down like rain.

The science is straightforward: the sun's heat causes the water to evaporate, turning from liquid to vapor. The vapor rises and condenses on the cooler surface of the bag, forming droplets. When the droplets get heavy enough, they fall back down, mimicking precipitation. This is a perfect introduction to the water cycle for younger students, but it can also be used for older students to discuss the role of solar energy and the states of matter. To extend the experiment, you can place one bag in a sunny window and another in a dark closet, then compare the rates of condensation.

The Upside-Down Glass: Air Pressure and Water

This next experiment seems to defy gravity and always elicits gasps from students. You will need a glass, a piece of stiff cardboard or a plastic plate, and water. Fill the glass completely to the brim with water. Place the cardboard over the top, ensuring it covers the entire opening. Hold the cardboard firmly against the glass, then quickly turn the glass upside down over a sink. Carefully remove your hand from the cardboard. The water stays in the glass!

This is a classic demonstration of air pressure. The water is held in the glass because the air pressure outside the glass is greater than the pressure inside. The cardboard acts as a barrier, preventing air from entering the glass. The weight of the water pushes down on the cardboard, but the air pressure pushing up on the cardboard is much stronger. This is a simple but powerful way to show that air has weight and exerts pressure.

For a more advanced twist, you can repeat the experiment with a piece of mesh or a thin cloth instead of cardboard. The water still stays in the glass because the surface tension of the water creates a seal across the tiny holes in the mesh. This combines the concepts of air pressure and surface tension in one dramatic demonstration.

Why Water is Weird: The Anomalous Expansion of Ice

Most substances contract when they cool and expand when they heat up. Water is the exception. It contracts as it cools down to 4 degrees Celsius, but then it begins to expand as it cools further to 0 degrees Celsius, where it freezes. This is why ice floats. If ice sank, our lakes and rivers would freeze from the bottom up, making aquatic life impossible in winter.

You can demonstrate this anomaly with a simple experiment. Fill a small, unbreakable container to the brim with water and seal it tightly. Place it in the freezer. When the water freezes, it will expand and may crack the container or push the lid off. This expansion is why water pipes burst in the winter.

To make this a more controlled experiment, you can use a graduated cylinder. Fill it to the 100 ml mark with water and note the exact level. Place it in the freezer and, after it freezes, observe the new level. You will see that the ice level is higher than the original water level. This is a direct measurement of expansion. This experiment is a great springboard for discussing density, molecular structure, and the unique properties that make water essential for life on Earth.

Conclusion

Water is not just the stuff of rivers and rain; it is a masterclass in physics and chemistry. From the cohesive forces that let water defy gravity in a glass to the anomalous expansion that keeps our planet habitable, the experiments in this article reveal the hidden science in every drop. These activities are more than just fun—they are the seeds of scientific thinking. They teach students to observe, hypothesize, and understand the world around them. So the next time you pour a glass of water, take a moment to appreciate the incredible forces at play. Then, grab some supplies and start experimenting.
